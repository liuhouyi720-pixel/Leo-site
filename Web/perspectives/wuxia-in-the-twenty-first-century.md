*Houyi Liu*

In Jin Yong's *Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils*, Qiao Feng spends years building up the Beggars' Sect. Then Quan Guanqing spreads rumors about him and Madam Ma and accuses him of colluding with the Liao. Just like that, the sect casts out its own leader. Meanwhile, Duan Yu and his father carry on with one woman after another and get away with it. I read the novel in middle school and figured some petty men simply felt threatened by Qiao Feng. Only now do I see what mattered. Qiao Feng was Khitan, a man of the Liao, in a world that expected him to be Han Chinese.

The world has had its fill of NVIDIA for a long time. Think of the Wall Street fund managers chasing each rally and selling into each dip, the AI companies desperate for clusters of GPUs and reluctant to depend on a single supplier, or the gamers I count myself among, whose voices have all but disappeared from the conversation. We all watched Jensen Huang build his empire, one floor at a time, in his signature leather jacket, calling out, "Buy more, save more." Few of us wanted a world with NVIDIA towering over everyone else. Even so, it was hard to watch things come to this.

I've never been a disciple of Team Green, though I've always admired its products. NVIDIA is a great company, and it owes much of that greatness to Jensen. People say NVIDIA rode the AI wave. I'd go further and say NVIDIA made today's AI possible. When Google introduced the Transformer, hardly anyone was talking about emergent abilities. BERT had its moment, and it still felt worlds away from what we now call large language models. Jensen tells a story about how NVIDIA spent billions developing a computer expressly for AI. Nobody wanted to buy it. The thing couldn't even run Windows. One San Francisco startup wanted it badly, but it was a nonprofit and couldn't afford the hardware. So Jensen gave it the machine. That startup was OpenAI.

You could say Jensen is the whole story of NVIDIA. He drove one change after another, inside the company and beyond it. He championed general-purpose computing with CUDA, doubled down on AI data centers, pushed into optical transceivers, and used NVLink to tie thousands of GPUs together. It's the story of a computing giant told in the most unabashedly heroic terms. I admire him from the outside. Imagine what it feels like to work there. I wonder how many people joined his ranks because they believed in him personally. Even the lowest-ranking soldier grows attached to a general who leads them to victory after victory. In this case, the victories opened up a new era in human civilization.

That's why I'm pessimistic about NVIDIA's future after Jensen. A century of corporate history and plenty of management research ask the same question. Why does a well-run company lose its footing when it changes CEOs, especially when a founder hands over the reins for the first time? I'm reminded of that line from the "General Kim" meme about how people who work in culture ought to have some culture. A tech CEO ought to understand technology. You can design incentives to keep a big company on its feet. Leading it through a transformation on the scale of what its founder did takes something else.

I think again of Qiao Feng. I'm happy and grateful to see the kind of heroism I grew up reading about in *wuxia*, the Chinese tradition of martial arts fiction, alive in the twenty-first century. I see an echo of Qiao Feng's Liao ancestry in the way Huang's Chinese heritage figures in his story. There's plenty of politics here that I feel ill-equipped to untangle. What I see in much of the Western coverage is a shameless double standard and a particularly cutting kind of prejudice. The glee at finding something to use against him, the speed with which people switched sides once the tree fell, the fury of left-wing outlets I see as Democratic Party mouthpieces as they realized AI speaks Chinese as readily as English. It seemed to me they wanted one scandal to wipe out everything he had achieved. It brought to mind the old saying that those who feast on meat lack the foresight to plan ahead. Here were Wall Street grandees and Washington dignitaries who, I suspect, had never read a paper on Transformers and couldn't tell you how a model generates a token, making a spectacle of a leather jacket and a man's sex life. It reminded me of Zhao Gao pointing at a deer and calling it a horse, and Qin Hui conjuring up charges out of thin air.

On November 23, 2026, the first thing I saw when I woke up was that Jensen was back as CEO of NVIDIA. It was the most surreal, gloriously over-the-top tale of individual heroism I'd ever seen. Even a novelist would think twice about writing this plot. Imagine you've been forced out of the company you built because of a mistake you made. The whole world is denouncing you. Then the most respected masters of the martial world speak up for you, and wandering swordsmen you've never met put their names to a petition. The disciples of your own sect are so outraged that they would give up their standing to follow you into exile. Three days after you ride through the mountain pass, the Central Plains are in chaos. Court officials, outlaws and upstanding citizens alike, even the ministers who publicly spat on your name, all bow and beg you to return. You knew all along that those nights and days running into each other when you were starting out, and those drinks you shared with leaders of rival sects on rainy nights far from home, had counted for something.

I can almost hear the hero's next line. *You know, Elon Musk is my best buddy. And President Trump? I'd call him my godfather.* In the video from NVIDIA headquarters that day, employees cheered as Jensen walked in. On Wall Street, NVIDIA shares climbed roughly 30 percent by midday. The market gave the king of semiconductors a welcome as honest as any he's likely to get. If this were a novel, revenge would come next. Part of me can't wait to see Jensen take on Anthropic, even though I know real life rarely follows the plot.

What made me so happy? There was the sheer drama of it, and the affinity I felt as someone of Chinese heritage myself. Above all, it was the first time in a while that I'd heard a story from the age of AI with a human being at its center. I don't know when it started, but it feels as though AI has been taking apart and rearranging the way I understand the world. The stories we hear seem to have stopped being about people. The more capable the models get, the easier it is to think that anyone could be replaced. If AI can come up with a proof for the Navier-Stokes problem, will we still need mathematicians? The fear of AI-driven unemployment hung over my entire semester in grad school. Then, for once, a story seemed to say that what made Jensen indispensable was the human part of him. He leaves and the contracts are all still there. So are the company, the org chart, and the chip designs. Why does it have to be him? Why do we still need a CEO when we could imagine an all-knowing agent that can solve the Riemann hypothesis? It was that moment in which something recognizably human mattered so much that moved me.

Maybe the next time I hit a setback, I'll think of what Jensen went through and find a little more courage and composure.
