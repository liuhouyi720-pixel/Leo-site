# Written Before Argentina vs. England

*Houyi Liu*

> I knew Argentina through its football before I knew it as a country.

## A Country I Met Through Football

I still remember the summer after my second year of middle school. Argentina had been eliminated, and I listened again and again to *Don't Cry for Me Argentina*. Its familiar melody carried a sadness that seemed older than the song itself. It made me want to understand the country from which such sorrow appeared to come.

Later, deceived - or perhaps guided - by *Cultural Journey*, I began to read Julio Cortázar's *The Southern Thruway*. Then, through the words of the football commentator He Wei, I found Borges.

## The Absurdity Beneath the Surface

In a documentary about Messi, I learned that although he had lived in Barcelona for more than twenty years, he had never lost his Rosario accent. I once read a comment saying that Argentines possessed so little that even an accent became one of the few things they could truly call their own, and for that reason they guarded it carefully.

Perhaps the remark was exaggerated. Yet there was something true in its sadness.

The same absurdity runs through *The Southern Thruway*. It also seems to belong to Argentina itself. People are trapped without explanation. They create habits, loyalties, hierarchies, and forms of tenderness inside a disaster whose beginning they cannot understand and whose end they cannot predict. What is difficult to accept is not that such things can be imagined, but that they can truly happen in the world.

## After the Traffic Starts Moving

Reading the story again reminded me of Camus's *The Plague*.

I do not wish to judge the justice of the Falklands War, nor to settle the merits and failures of Peronism. What concerns me is something simpler and more difficult. When the traffic suddenly begins to move, when the disaster ends as inexplicably as it began, how does one return to the ordinary world? How does one live again in a society reshaped by absurdity, carrying the wounds left by alienation and suffering?

This is not only Argentina's question.

Sooner or later, it becomes everyone's.

## A Tango Against the Void

Camus believed that, in the face of the absurd, a human being must continue to perform the most basic duties of being human: to remain honest and to preserve compassion.

For Argentina, perhaps football serves a similar purpose.

In it, a country gathers its defeats, its pride, its grief, and its impossible hopes. It dances not because the world has offered an answer, but because silence is unbearable.

Argentina's devotion to football is its tragic tango against the void.
