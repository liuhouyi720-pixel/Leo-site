# From New York to Champaign

*Houyi Liu*

> I may not be missing an apartment or even a group of friends. What I miss is the finest year of my youth, the one I spent in New York.

## One Week Away

It has been a week since I left my home in New Jersey and moved temporarily to Champaign. Much of the disorientation I felt when I arrived has eased. Life is gradually finding a rhythm and beginning to look up. Yet once the thought of returning to China enters my mind, I cannot completely erase it.

## Why New York Was Different

I love New York. That is not simply nostalgia, nor is it only because I lived there long enough to become attached. I am a nostalgic person. Although I have described myself as adaptable on more than one personality test for job applications, whenever I enter a new environment I cannot help missing the place before it. I felt it when I began boarding school after elementary school, and again when I began commuting during my final year of high school so that I could study more effectively.

New York somehow broke that pattern. When I moved from Chengdu to New York, I felt no discomfort at all. The city fascinated me from the beginning. There were brief moments of self-doubt caused by my limited English, but throughout the 365 days I lived there, there was not a single day when I especially missed Chengdu.

Perhaps that was simply because life in New York was too good.

I said I had come to study abroad, but the year was far removed from what studying abroad usually means. It felt more like an extended stay after the end of my junior year. There was no pressure over grades and no awkward struggle to build a social life. I was surrounded by friends from the same university major. I did not have to learn how to live entirely on my own. My roommate was already a friend, the program had chosen our courses, and even most of my classmates belonged to the same project.

## The First Goodbye

That beautiful illusion began to break as I left New York. More precisely, it ended with graduation.

I first felt homesick on the day my roommate, Jiangjun, left. His flight was at ten in the morning, and he needed to leave at five, so we decided to stay awake all night. The atmosphere was strange. Yang, usually the most pragmatic and playful person in the room, became serious and began talking about life. We ate Qiaolezi ice-cream bars and joked about the education adviser Zhang Xuefeng. Thinking about it now, the contrast feels painfully apt: after high school, you can ask a famous adviser to help choose a university major; after college, no one is there to choose your life. In truth, no one can choose either your major or the life that follows it for you.

By three in the morning, everyone was tired, so on a whim we went out for McDonald's. Jersey City was quiet in the middle of the night. Across the water, Manhattan was still glittering, though fewer windows were lit than before. It was already June, but the Atlantic wind remained cool after dark. I knew that, for some of my friends, this might be their last night in New York.

After seeing them off at dawn, I slept until the afternoon. When I woke, the sun was already sinking. Light poured through my window, which faced Midtown, and above the Empire State Building the young moon was already visible. Instinctively, I stood and walked toward my roommate's room to ask what we should eat that evening. Only then did I remember that he had left ten hours earlier and was probably somewhere over the Pacific, flying home.

Sunlight filled his completely empty room. Every grain of dust drifting through its path became sharply visible. Something in me tightened. Once again, I called my father and asked whether I should come home.

## The Last Days of New York

The days that followed were very "full." As a child, I used to treasure the final days of every school holiday; now I treasured my last days with New York in the same way. I went from Coney Island to the Guggenheim, from 23rd Street, which I had walked countless times, to 42nd Street, where only tourists seemed to go. The Knicks had just won a championship and the World Cup had arrived, making an already energetic city even more feverish.

During those two weeks, except for the moments when exhaustion forced me to stop and anxiety about the uncertain future returned, I was very happy. Time rushed forward, and the day of departure drew closer.

## Along the Hudson

On the afternoon of the fifteenth, I packed my first box and took it to Newport to ship. Afterward, I walked along the Hudson. The sunlight was perfect, and people were lying beside the river. Across the water, the glass of One World Trade Center reflected the light moving across the surface.

I thought again of my second day in New York. I had gone to Newport to meet Mr. Huang. After lunch and a conversation about opening a bank account, we walked to the river and I saw the full skyline of Lower Manhattan for the first time. I was filled then with visions of the year ahead. Looking at those tall office buildings across the water, I imagined that one day I might earn a place in this city. The road would be difficult, but if I could wake each day to the river and the distant figure of the Statue of Liberty, everything would feel worthwhile. Everything would still be worth anticipating.

## Moving Out

I spent my final night packing. Brother Feng and I carried our mattresses and the rest of our large furniture to an apartment near Journal Square to throw them away. The scene resembled our first day in America. Once again, we were moving back and forth between Urby and other buildings after a sleepless night, hauling heavy mattresses and looking equally disheveled. The difference was that, on our first day, we had carried the excitement of entering a new world and the promise of the future. Now we carried our reluctance to leave an old place and our uncertainty about whatever came next.

The instant of farewell was not the hardest part. Perhaps the previous two weeks had prepared me for it. It was the long process of leaving that tested me. After we hugged, the elevator doors on the twenty-first floor closed slowly. My roommate of one year and my friend of four years disappeared from view. The woman at the front desk said goodbye too. She told me I would surely return to New York.

I stepped out of the building and onto the subway. The city was rushing as it always did. I was so tired that I fell asleep as soon as I sat down on the plane. My final glimpse of New York was the East River, seen from the jet bridge at LaGuardia Airport.

## One Week in Champaign

After a week in Champaign, I cannot yet say that I have come to love the place. In theory, as an INFJ - one of those green old souls in the MBTI universe - I should enjoy a quiet town where I can do my own work without interruption.

In fact, I do like the university. It has historic buildings, an enormous campus, generous green space, serviceable but unremarkable facilities, and, best of all, a strong sports culture. People meet less frequently here than they do in New York, but perhaps the connections between them are stronger. Students and staff in UIUC clothing are everywhere. Unlike New York's almost Lovecraftian complexity, relationships here seem comparatively simple.

Perhaps my initial discomfort did not mean that I disliked the city or the university. Perhaps I was simply nostalgic, reluctant to leave the boundaries of my comfort zone.

## Missing a Time, Not a Place

I like the feeling of being on the road. On every personality test, I say that I do not want a life in which nothing changes. I want serendipities. Yet whenever my surroundings change, I cannot help looking backward.

A new friend in Champaign asked why I had not stayed in New York for another two months. Friends in New York told me that, if I truly could not bear it, I could return to China or come back to New York. But I know that going back to New York would not make me feel much better. The scenery would be familiar, but almost all the friends who once shared it with me have left.

I may not be missing an apartment or even a group of friends. What I miss is the finest year of my youth, the one I spent in New York.

That full year was held up by the Manhattan skyline during the summer solstice, by a girl's laughter on the twenty-fifth floor late at night, by being trapped inside during a snowstorm and the glorious PUBG victories I shared with my roommates, and by an elderly professor's classroom filled with the smell of coffee. We cannot truly feel the beauty of a moment until it has become the past. A street dancer performing "Forever Young" on the white sand of Tampa seemed to embody this entire year in America.

The winter application season was anxious, but from here it no longer seems so terrible. I am anxious now about jobs and internships, but perhaps one day that anxiety will also seem small. Some happy moments, however, will remain happy forever.

I miss the year when friends were beside me day and night and nothing seemed to weigh on us, because I know I may never have another time quite like it. Even if I return to New York, the twenty-four-year-old version of me will find a different city. The song may be the same, but the listener will not be.

## Why Not Go Home?

The second question - why not return to China? - is more complicated.

As a child, when I encountered the old line of poetry about growing more timid as one approaches home, I could not understand it. Homecoming, I thought, must always be joyful. Only now do I understand how complicated human feeling can be. Sometimes the anxiety of having accomplished nothing, wrapped around my proud and fragile self-respect, overpowers even my longing for home.

I have never seen *Inside Llewyn Davis*, but its music video moves me every time I open it. One Valentine's night, I drank alone at home, mixing cola with a little rum and gin. Sitting on my bed, I looked toward Midtown Manhattan, glowing pink in the distance, while "Five Hundred Miles" played on my computer.

I was less envious of other couples enjoying sweet dates than troubled by a larger question: in this immense city of neon and glamour, could there be a place for me? When the song's penniless traveler admits that he cannot go home, tears gathered in my eyes.

The same feeling returned in Champaign. On my second and most difficult day there, I went to the Illini Union to watch the United States play in the World Cup. At the end of the match, the entire team thanked the crowd. "Take Me Home, Country Roads" filled Seattle's Lumen Field, and the spectators sang together. Far away in Champaign, I began to miss that rainy city in southwestern China.
